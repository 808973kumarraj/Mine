Say your data won't fit on one computer. Not even close.
So you split it into pieces and spread them across many machines.
Apache Spark is the engine that runs your code on all those pieces at once. It started at UC Berkeley in 2009.
Every Spark app has one boss: the driver. It runs your code and makes the plan.
The workers are executors. They do the actual work and hold data in memory.
A cluster manager, like YARN or Kubernetes, hands out the machines.
Let's count words. First, Spark splits the text into partitions. One task per partition.
Now we write map and reduceByKey. Here's the twist: nothing runs yet.
Transformations are lazy. Spark just writes them down, like a recipe.
Only an action, like collect, says: go. Now Spark looks at the whole recipe and plans.
The plan is a graph, split into stages. The split happens at a shuffle.
Splitting words can happen right where the data lives. No talking needed.
But counting "cat" means every "cat" has to meet in one place. That's the shuffle.
Shuffles cost disk, network, and time. Fewer shuffles, faster jobs.
Spark can keep data in memory between steps, instead of writing to disk every time.
For jobs that loop over the same data, the original paper measured up to 20 times faster than Hadoop.
Use DataFrames or SQL, and an optimizer called Catalyst rewrites your plan before it runs.
And if a machine dies? Spark doesn't keep copies. It keeps the recipe.
It replays the steps for just the lost piece, and the job carries on.
So: a driver plans, executors work, shuffles split stages, memory keeps it fast.
Nothing runs until you ask. And when you do, Spark already knows the fastest way there.
