Notes

What the notebook teaches

The Energy Ledger covers the energy topic in GCSE and IGCSE Physics (for example AQA GCSE Physics 8463, section 4.1, and Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625, topic 1.7). It suits ages 14 to 16 and takes about 30 to 40 minutes.

  • Energy is held in stores (gravitational, kinetic, chemical, thermal) and moves between them.
  • The total never changes: energy is conserved.
  • Some energy always spreads out into the surroundings. It is still there, but much harder to use.
  • Efficiency compares the useful energy out with the total energy in.

How the ledger works. Each coin is one joule. When energy moves from one store to another, a coin flies between the stacks, so students can count the transfer. On every page the stacks add up to the same total. Each page asks for a guess before students try the model, which brings out what they already think.

1. The swing

Big idea: energy moves back and forth between the gravitational and kinetic stores.

Ballm = 2 kg, on a 2 m string, g = 10 N/kg
Pulled to 60°h = L(1 − cos θ) = 2 × (1 − 0.5) = 1.00 m
Gravitational storeEp = m g h = 2 × 10 × 1 = 20 J
Speed at the bottom½ m v² = 20 J, so v = √20 ≈ 4.5 m/s

Common mistake: "the swing slows down because the energy gets used up." With air resistance on, the coins move into the thermal store of the air. None disappear.

Questions to ask

  1. Pull the ball back to 30°. How high is it lifted, and how much energy is stored? h = 2 × (1 − cos 30°) ≈ 0.27 m, so about 5.4 J.
  2. Why does the ball not rise higher on the other side than where it started? It only has the energy it started with.
  3. With the air on, where has the energy gone after a minute? Into the thermal store of the air, which is very slightly warmer.

2. Slide and stop

Big idea: friction does work on the sledge and moves its energy into the thermal store. Work done = force × distance.

Sledgem = 2 kg at the top of a 1 m high slope: 20 J
Speed at the bottomv = √(2 g h) ≈ 4.5 m/s (the slope has no friction)
Plasticfriction 5 N, stops after 4 m: 5 × 4 = 20 J
Carpetfriction 10 N, stops after 2 m: 10 × 2 = 20 J
Sandpaperfriction 20 N, stops after 1 m: 20 × 1 = 20 J

Common mistake: "the rough mat makes more heat." Every mat ends with the same 20 J of thermal energy. A rough mat only does it in a shorter distance.

Questions to ask

  1. A new mat gives a friction force of 8 N. How far does the sledge slide? 20 J ÷ 8 N = 2.5 m.
  2. What would change if the slope also had friction? Some thermal energy is made on the slope, so the sledge reaches the mat with less than 20 J and stops sooner.
  3. Where exactly does the thermal energy end up? In the mat, the runners and, a little later, the air around them.

3. Light and heat

Big idea: a torch moves energy from the battery's chemical store. The electric current carries it to the bulb, and it leaves as light and by heating. Efficiency = useful energy out ÷ total energy in.

Battery20 J left (a nearly flat cell, so the numbers stay small)
Filament bulbabout 2 J of light, 18 J of heating: efficiency 2 ÷ 20 = 10%
LEDabout 6 J of light, 14 J of heating: efficiency 6 ÷ 20 = 30%

Common mistake: "light is a store." Light is a way energy travels (radiation). It ends up warming whatever it lands on, here the wall.

Questions to ask

  1. Why does an LED feel cooler to touch than a filament bulb? Less of its energy goes into heating.
  2. The torch uses 2.6 W. How long do the last 20 J last? 20 ÷ 2.6 ≈ 7.7 s.
  3. A fresh AA cell stores roughly 15,000 J. How long could it run this torch? 15,000 ÷ 2.6 ≈ 5,800 s, about 1.6 hours (less at high current).

4. Spread thin

Big idea: dissipation. The 20 J spread into the thermal store of the whole room. They are still there, but too spread out to be useful.

Room air4 m × 4 m × 2.5 m = 40 m³, at 1.2 kg/m³ ≈ 48 kg
Heat capacityabout 1,000 J per kg per °C, so ≈ 48,000 J per °C
Temperature rise20 J ÷ 48,000 J/°C ≈ 0.0004 °C

Questions to ask

  1. The energy is still in the room. Why can't it light the bulb again? It is spread over a huge amount of air and wall at almost the same temperature, so there is no easy way to collect it.
  2. Give two ways to waste less energy in a torch. Use an LED, and switch it off when it isn't needed.

5. The books balance

A short summary page. A good exit question: "Using energy stores, explain why a playground swing slowly stops if nobody pushes it."

Simplifications to point out

  • We use g = 10 N/kg. On Earth it is about 9.8 N/kg.
  • The swing's air resistance is a simple drag that grows with speed.
  • The sledge's slope has no friction, and we ignore air resistance on it.
  • The friction forces were chosen to give round numbers.
  • The battery is imagined almost flat (20 J) so the numbers stay small.
  • Efficiencies are rounded. Real bulbs vary.
  • The room estimate only warms the air. Walls and furniture soak up energy too, so the real rise is even smaller.
  • Coins are whole joules, so each stack is rounded to the nearest coin. The total is always exact.

Where the numbers come from

  • Gravity. Standard gravity is defined as exactly 9.80665 m/s². This book rounds it to 10 N/kg, as some exam boards do. Wikipedia: Standard gravity
  • The swing. Height lifted h = L(1 − cos θ), and the period of a pendulum T = 2π√(L/g), which gives about 2.8 s for a 2 m string. HyperPhysics: Simple pendulum
  • Friction. Sliding friction coefficients for everyday materials are mostly between about 0.2 and 0.6. The book's 5 N, 10 N and 20 N (coefficients 0.25, 0.5 and 1.0 for a 2 kg sledge) were picked for round numbers. Engineering ToolBox: Friction coefficients
  • Filament bulbs. About 10% of the energy leaves as visible light. Measured against the eye's sensitivity (luminous efficiency), it is only about 2 to 5%. Either way, most of it heats the surroundings. Penn State MRSEC: Light bulb facts, Exploratorium: Invisible light, Wikipedia: Incandescent light bulb
  • LEDs. Typical white LEDs turn about 20 to 30% of their electrical energy into light. The best lab devices reach about 40%. The book uses 30%. Japanese Journal of Applied Physics 45, L1084 (2006), Wikipedia: Luminous efficacy
  • Batteries. A typical alkaline AA cell is rated about 2,850 mAh at 1.5 V, about 4.3 Wh or 15,000 J, and gives less at high current. 20 J is a nearly flat cell. Wikipedia: Alkaline battery
  • Air. Air at 20 °C has a density of about 1.2 kg/m³ and a specific heat capacity of about 1.0 kJ per kg per °C. Wikipedia: Density of air, Engineering ToolBox: Air specific heat
  • Syllabus. AQA GCSE Physics 8463
